SAMPLE MANUSCRIPT — invented characters and scenes for demonstrating the skill.

Scene 1 — March 2023 (Inés is 31; Marek is 38)
Harbor Street Tavern, Portland, ME

Inés had come to the trivia night to meet her cousin's friends, and she was already regretting it. The table was too small, the music too loud, and the man across from her had not touched his pint since sitting down.

"Marek," her cousin had said by way of introduction. "He's good at trivia. Don't ask him for anything else, he gets weird."

Marek nodded at her. He was tall and narrow, with sandy hair going gray early at the temples, and a habit of keeping both hands flat on the table where she could see them, as if he'd been asked to. Everyone else at the table called him Mac. He did not explain it.

"Hospital scheduling," he said, when she asked what he did. "Boring. Spreadsheets mostly."

She might have believed it, but she'd noticed him sitting with his back to the wall, facing the door and the bar both. When the waitress dropped a tray in the far corner, glass shattering, every head at the table turned. Marek's turned too, but a half-second later than hers, and only after his eyes had already gone to the waitress's hands, then her feet, then the glass.

"She's fine," he said quietly, before anyone asked. "Didn't cut herself. Sit, she's got it."

The waitress was fine. She was already sweeping.

Later Inés saw him watching the man two tables over, a heavyset guy in a Red Sox jersey who'd gone pale and sweaty over his wings. Marek had angled his chair slightly. He was counting something under his breath, his lips barely moving. Eight, nine, ten.

"Your friend okay?" Inés asked.

"Probably," said Marek. "Get him some water, maybe. And tell him to sit up straight."

The man in the jersey took a long breath, grumbled, and drank the water his friend slid over. The color came back to his face a minute later. Marek exhaled and picked up his glass for the first time all night. He still didn't drink from it.

Scene 2 — April 2023
Inés's apartment, Munjoy Hill

He had come for dinner, and by nine o'clock he was asleep on her couch with his shoes on. Inés stood in the doorway holding a dish towel, unsure whether to wake him.

When she shook his shoulder he came awake instantly, no groan, no drifting up out of sleep, just eyes open and already focused, as if he'd been flipped on like a switch. "What time is it? Did something happen?"

"Nothing happened. You fell asleep."

"Sorry." He rubbed his face. "I've been up since four yesterday afternoon. I forget what a regular day feels like."

"Hospital scheduling," she said, a little dryly.

"Mm." He looked at his hands. They were dry and cracked across the knuckles, the skin raw from scrubbing, with a faint bruise on the inside of his left elbow where a vein had been used for something. He saw her looking and turned them over. "Winter."

It was April.

At the kitchen counter she cut her thumb opening a can, a shallow slice that bled more than it should. Before she had finished swearing he was beside her, a clean towel pressed against it, his fingers settled lightly at her wrist without any apparent thought.

"Hold it above your heart. Higher. Look at me, not at it." His voice had dropped half a register, slow and even, the voice of a person who had said these words to many frightened strangers. "You're okay. It's shallow. It looks worse than it is. Tell me what you had for lunch."

"What?"

"Lunch. Just tell me."

She did, and by the time she'd finished, the bleeding had slowed and her pulse had stopped racing. He peeled back the towel, studied the cut, and nodded. "Butterfly strip, not a stitch. Have you got a first aid kit?"

She didn't. He looked genuinely pained by this, the most expressive he'd been all evening.

Scene 3 — July 2023
A cookout at Inés's cousin's place, Falmouth

The kid went down near the volleyball net, a thin boy of about nine who had been running and then wasn't. Someone screamed. Inés turned and saw Marek already moving, dropping his plate on the grass, already crouched at the boy's head before most of the adults had registered what had happened.

"Not breathing right," he said, to no one and everyone. "You, in the blue, call 911, tell them a child is down, possible seizure, we're at"—he rattled off the cross streets without looking up—"and stay on the line. You, hold his legs, gently. Don't put anything in his mouth. Nobody crowd him."

He was calm as a held breath. He turned the boy onto his side, checked his airway with two fingers, and watched the boy's chest with an intent, unblinking attention, counting under his breath again, eight, nine, ten, until the twitching stopped.

"There you go," he told the child, who was blinking up at him in confusion. "Welcome back. Don't try to get up. You did great. Can you tell me your name?"

By the time the ambulance arrived, he had a clear sequence to give them without notes: when it began, how long it lasted, what the boy had eaten, which of the adults was the mother. The paramedics, a woman and a younger man, nodded at him with the specific ease of people recognizing one of their own.

"Anything else?" the woman asked.

"Pupils equal and reactive. Breathing's fine now. He's postictal, he'll be foggy." He pulled a long breath. "Bring him to Maine Med, they've got the pediatric neuro."

Afterward he stood alone at the edge of the lawn, scrubbing his hands with sanitizer from his pocket, over and over, until Inés touched his arm. He flinched, and then smiled at her, tired.

"I'm fine. It's just that I don't usually get to see how they end."

Scene 4 — November 2023
Marek's kitchen, South Portland

She finally saw his apartment. It was spare: a mattress with blackout curtains, a rack of drying scrubs on the shower rod, a single mug in the sink. Three different pairs of the same sturdy clogs sat by the door, each worn to a different degree.

"You work too much for someone who does scheduling," she said.

He gave her a long look. "I work nights," he said at last, "and I'm not good at explaining it to people who don't." He took a breath. "I'm good at the part where it's chaos and I know what to do. I'm bad at the part afterward, when I go home and nothing's wrong."

She waited.

"I told you it was scheduling because the other answer makes people want to tell me about their own bad hospital experiences or ask what's wrong with their knee." He nearly smiled. "Mostly I just wanted to be someone who was off the clock."

"And now?"

"Now you've seen me do my actual job on a volleyball court, so." He shrugged, and for the first time since she'd met him, he took his hands off the table, off the counter, out of sight, and let them rest in his lap like a person who no longer had anything to prove.
